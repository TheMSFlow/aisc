# Instagram Script Guide — 2 to 3 minute vertical

> Read `agent-guides/blog/BLOG_BRIEF.md` and `agent-guides/blog/SOCIAL_GUIDE.md` first.
> The voice rules in `agent-guides/COPY_GUIDE.md` apply to every word Michael says on camera. Forbidden Phrases table and the em-dash ban included. A script is spoken copy, not exempt copy.

**Settled 2026-08-13.** One published briefing becomes one 2 to 3 minute portrait video for Instagram. This is the **primary video asset**, not a cut-down of anything: YouTube long-form is paused on equipment (`SOCIAL_GUIDE.md` § YouTube is paused on equipment), so the vertical is originated straight from the briefing.

**This is a true script, word for word.** That is a deliberate break from the session-notes rule, which forbids scripts and allows only cues. The break is justified by runtime: at 15 minutes, reading kills the delivery and there is room to find your way into a point. At 3 minutes there is no room to wander, the first second decides whether anyone stays, and the compression a vertical needs only comes from writing the actual sentences. Do not "fix" this back to cue notes.

---

## The Budget

Conversational delivery to camera runs 140 to 155 words per minute. That fixes the arithmetic:

| Runtime | Words |
|---|---|
| 2:00 | ~290 |
| 2:30 | ~360 |
| 3:00 | ~440 |

**Target 340 to 400 words.** Under 290 the piece is a Reel, not a briefing, and it will not carry a reframe. Over 440 it stops being a vertical.

**A briefing is 1,000 to 1,800 words. The script is roughly a fifth of it.** This is the whole editorial problem and it is the opposite of the session-notes problem. Session notes need an expansion prompt because a briefing read aloud runs short of 15 minutes. A script needs ruthless subtraction because a briefing read aloud runs four times too long.

**So: one point. Not five.** Pick the single sharpest reframe in the briefing and give it away completely. Everything that is not that point, or not load-bearing for it, is cut. A vertical that covers three points covers none of them.

## The Shape

Six beats. Timings are indicative, word counts are the real constraint.

| Beat | Time | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **STOP** | 0:00–0:05 | 12–20 | One sentence naming the viewer's situation. Stops the scroll |
| **BELIEF** | 0:05–0:30 | 50–65 | What the leader currently assumes. Stated straight, with respect |
| **TURN** | 0:30–1:20 | 110–130 | The reframe. What is actually true. This is the video |
| **COST** | 1:20–2:05 | 85–105 | What believing the old thing costs. One concrete example or number |
| **MOVE** | 2:05–2:35 | 55–70 | One thing to do. Doable this week. The native embed lives here |
| **CLOSE** | 2:35–2:50 | 25–40 | The decision, then where the briefing lives |

### STOP

**A scene with a sting, never a proposition.** Sharpened 2026-08-13: the weakest beat across the first seven was consistently this one, and the strongest example shows why. "Your people are asking about AI. In the car home, at the kitchen table. Just not you." works because it puts the viewer somewhere specific and then turns on them. "Your organization keeps a list of things it does not offer" is a proposition: true, relevant, and inert.

Two parts, in this order:

1. **A concrete moment**, with at least one detail the viewer can picture.
2. **The sting**: the short clause that makes it their problem. Usually four words or fewer, and it lands last.

**There is no click to confirm, so click confirmation does not apply.** Instagram autoplays in-feed. The viewer has made no decision to defend and no expectation to meet; they are mid-scroll and the video is already running. What has to happen in the first second is a **scroll-stop**, and it is a harder problem than a click-confirm, because a click-confirm gets to reward a choice the viewer already made.

Four rules:

1. **Open on the viewer's situation, never on the video.** Not "today we're talking about", not "in this video", not a greeting, not his name. Same rule as a briefing's first paragraph: name something the viewer recognizes from their own week.
2. **It must match the cover.** The cover is this video's poster frame, and the cover headline is the nearest thing to a title. If the cover says one thing and the first line opens on another, the poster has mis-sold the video. Build the script against the cover headline, or rebuild the cover.
3. **Write it to be read, not only heard.** Most Instagram viewing starts muted. The STOP line is also the on-screen caption and often the only copy a scroller ever takes in. If it does not work silently on a phone screen, it does not work.
4. **No windup.** Any words spent arriving at the point are words spent losing the viewer.

### BELIEF

State the conventional take on the topic, the one the viewer probably holds. This is Callaway's move and it transfers cleanly: people listen once they believe you are talking about something they actually care about, and the contrast in the TURN needs something to contrast against.

**State it with respect, not as a straw man.** The audience is chiefs. A belief set up only to be knocked down insults the person holding it, and a leader can feel the setup coming. The version to write is the one an intelligent person holds for good reasons.

**And describe what they actually do, not a tidy version of it.** Added 2026-08-13. One script had leaders "reading the capability lists and watching the demos", which is how almost nobody encounters AI. They hear it from a peer at dinner, get a vendor deck they did not ask for, watch a teenager use it, read one headline. Write the real behaviour. A viewer who does not recognise themselves in BELIEF stops watching before the TURN.

### TURN

The single sharpest reframe in the briefing, delivered in full.

**Give it away completely. Do not withhold the payoff to hold attention.** This is where the house rule overrides the source guide. `SOCIAL_GUIDE.md` § The Post already settled it for written posts: standalone value, not a trailer, teasers train an audience to scroll. It holds for video. A viewer who watches to the end and never clicks should still have gotten the thing.

The curiosity-loop machinery from creator-economy script advice is built for watch time on a platform paid by watch time. Michael is paid by trust. A senior viewer who notices they are being strung along does not admire the technique.

**Per-point structure, adapted from the value loop:** say what it is, plainly. Say what it looks like in the viewer's own week. Say why it matters at their altitude. Base fact first, then application, then framing.

### The one re-hook

Exactly one, at the seam between TURN and COST. Not four, and not one per beat: at this length a stack of re-hooks reads as manipulation rather than pacing.

It is a bridge, not a tease. The honest form names why the next thing is load-bearing for the thing just said, rather than promising a payoff and withholding it.

### COST

What it costs to keep operating on the old belief. **One concrete thing**, not a list: a number, a scenario, or a decision that goes wrong. Abstraction dies on camera faster than anywhere else.

Where a briefing carries a real figure, use it, and carry its date anchor exactly as the briefing does (`RESEARCH_GUIDE.md`). A fabricated statistic said out loud is worse than one in text, because it travels without its source attached and cannot be footnoted.

### MOVE

One action, small enough to do this week, specific enough that the viewer knows whether they did it.

**The native embed lives here and nowhere else.** Frame the pain the action only partly solves, then name the briefing or the challenge as what solves the rest, then move on. No ad break, no gear change, no "and by the way".

**The link is named here too, once, and never again. Settled 2026-08-13.** The first seven scripts mentioned the briefing in MOVE and then gave the path thirty seconds later in the CLOSE, so the reference dangled at the moment it was made and the close ended on a URL instead of on the argument. Attach the path to the reference: "the full version is in the briefing, linked in my bio." Then the CLOSE lands the decision and stops.

One destination, stated once, at the point the viewer is told there is more. A second mention is not reinforcement, it is a second CTA.

This is the source guide's best mechanic and it agrees with the house rule that AISC gets named where it genuinely answers the question raised, not on schedule. If it does not genuinely answer the question this briefing raised, leave it out. A script with no CTA beats a script with a bolted-on one.

**Proof is not credentials.** Callaway's proof beat is follower counts. Michael's proof is that the video described the viewer's actual seat with more precision than they expected, plus the cohorts where he has watched it play out. Never a follower count, never a view count, never "I've worked with hundreds of".

### CLOSE

The decision the reframe puts in front of the viewer. Nothing else.

**No link and no sign-off here**, because MOVE already gave the path. The last words out of Michael's mouth should be the argument landing, not a destination. Captions carry no clickable link anyway; the bio link at `michaelsteve.com/links` carries `utm_source=instagram` per `SOCIAL_GUIDE.md` § Link Tracking, updated per post.

End on direction, not summary. Same rule as a briefing's close. Never recap what was just said in a video this short: the viewer has not forgotten.

**Loop check, optional.** Reels reward rewatches, and a close whose last line runs plausibly into the STOP line earns them for free. Take it when it arrives naturally. Never contort the close to manufacture it.

## Plain language is the whole job

**Added 2026-08-13, after the first seven scripts.** This is the rule that separates a script that lands from one that sounds like it means something.

**Distinctiveness belongs to the idea. Familiarity belongs to the words.** A reframe should be one nobody else is making. The vocabulary carrying it should be the plainest available. When the two conflict, the plain word wins, because a listener gets one pass at a sentence and cannot re-read it.

| Fault | Example from the first seven | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Unresolved referent** | "the same people hold both, and neither one is you" | Name them. "Right now that mandate belongs to whoever is quietly experimenting" |
| **Vague verb doing load-bearing work** | "What surfaces will be bigger than you expect" | "What people tell you will be more than you expect" |
| **Unfamiliar metaphor** | "That question has no bottom" | "That question never ends" |
| **Abstract noun as the payload** | "the formation is happening either way" | Say what is actually happening to actual people |

**The test: read the sentence to someone outside the topic and ask them to say it back.** If they hesitate, the words are wrong, even when the idea is right.

Two things this rule does **not** license: dumbing the argument down, and dropping the register. The audience is chiefs. Plain is not casual, and simple words can carry a hard idea. The briefings themselves are the model, and where a briefing uses a phrase that is written-clever rather than spoken-clear, the script translates it rather than quoting it.

## Varying the shape, so seven scripts do not sound like one

The six beats are fixed. Everything inside them should not be, and the first seven scripts drifted toward a single template: same kind of opening, same kind of cost, same cadence.

**STOP openings. Rotate them, and record which one a script used** in its header as `**STOP form:**`. The first seven each got a different one, and reading them back to back is the proof that the rotation is doing real work:

| Type | Shape | Shipped in |
|---|---|---|
| The scene | A moment from their week, with one specific detail | Row 1, governance |
| The count | A number from their own life, not a statistic | Row 2, territory |
| The coming moment | Something that is about to be asked of them | Row 3, board |
| The flat contradiction | Two facts together that should not both be true | Row 4, service |
| The scene, from the other side | The same room seen by someone they are responsible for | Row 6, students |
| The overheard request | A real thing somebody asks them, and they stall on | Row 7, delegation |
| The relief nobody saw | A moment where not being asked was a relief | Row 8, clarity |
| The question you cannot answer | A fair question about their own week that they cannot honestly answer | Row 10, margin |
| The pitch that already reached them | Name the sales pitch they have heard, then concede it is true | Row 13, agents |
| The private conclusion | Say the thing they have decided about themselves, then contradict it | Row 14, margin |
| The manager you remember | A person from their own past, then the turn onto them | Row 15, briefing |

**Never repeat the previous script's form**, and check the two before it as well. Six forms is enough that nothing needs reusing inside a run.

**The best of these pay themselves off later in the script.** Row 7 opens on someone asking whether AI can write the performance reviews, and the TURN answers it: "performance reviews, since you asked, are high on both." Row 6 opens on a student between two classrooms and the CLOSE returns to that same student. An opening that gets answered turns the script into one object rather than six beats in a row.

**COST forms**, same rule: a scenario that goes wrong, a figure with its date anchor, a decision that gets made without them, or a thing someone else takes.

**Never invent an anecdote, a client, or a cohort story to create variety.** That is fabrication and it is the one line this system cannot cross. Variety comes from the *form* of the beat and from which true detail in the briefing gets the spotlight, never from making something up. The genuinely unique material, Michael's own cases, lives in the session-notes expansion prompts, where he supplies it himself.

## Voice, on top of COPY_GUIDE

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| No "today we're talking about", no "in this video", no "hey guys" | Creator-vlog register. Wrong room |
| No follower counts, view counts, or client counts as proof | See MOVE. Proof is precision about the seat |
| Contractions are correct here | Written copy and spoken copy diverge. "You are not going to" reads fine and sounds stilted |
| Short sentences. One clause where two would do | Every sentence has to survive being heard once, with no rereading |
| Read it out loud before shipping it | The only reliable test. Anything that trips the tongue gets rewritten |
| No em-dashes | Standing rule, and doubly so here: there is no spoken em-dash, so one in the script is a beat nobody can perform |

## Writing One

1. **Read the briefing.** Only published briefings, same rule as covers.
2. **Find the one reframe.** Usually the briefing's pull quote or its sharpest sentence. It is often the same line the cover headline was mined from, and that is a good sign, not a duplication.
3. **Check the cover.** If a cover already exists for this briefing, the STOP line answers to its headline. If it does not exist yet, note the headline the script implies so the cover agent inherits it.
4. **Draft to the beat table**, watching the word budget per beat rather than the total. Beats that run long steal from MOVE, which is where the conversion lives.
5. **Cut to 340–400 words.** First drafts run long. Cut whole sentences, never trim adverbs: compression comes from dropping ideas, not from tightening prose that should not be there.
6. **Read it aloud, timed.** If it lands over 3:00, cut again. Do not read faster.
7. **Self-check** against COPY_GUIDE's Forbidden Phrases table and the voice rules above.

## Storage

**Source and deliverable live apart. Settled 2026-08-13.**

```
content/sources/<slug>/script.md                       tracked in git
content/social/<publish-date>_<cover-id>_<slug>/
  <publish-date>_SCRIPT_<cover-id>_<slug>.docx         gitignored, ships
```

The markdown is the source of truth and belongs in version control: it is editorial work no command can regenerate, and it survives a fresh clone. The docx is generated from it and is what actually gets read on camera, because a phone or teleprompter app will not render markdown. **Never edit the docx.** Edit the markdown and rebuild:

```
node scripts/script-doc.mjs <slug>
```

The converter sets spoken copy at 15pt and everything else at 10pt grey, because the document is read while a camera runs and the two must never look alike. It fails on an em-dash, the same way the session-notes builder does.

**Nothing is uploaded and nothing is deleted.** Michael moves the `content/social/` folder to Drive himself.

Sources do **not** go in `content/awakening/`: the post loader at `src/lib/blog/posts.js:26` reads `.md` as well as `.mdx`, so a script dropped in there would be parsed as a briefing and fail the build.

## Source note

The framework here draws on *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked* (Callaway, transcript in `content/`), specifically: expectations versus reality as the governing principle, stating the common belief before contradicting it, the context/application/framing loop inside a point, and native embeds for the CTA.

It rejects, deliberately: click confirmation (no click exists in a Reels feed), the "second-best point first" ordering rule (needs four or more points to operate, and this format has one), curiosity loops that withhold the payoff (loses to the house standalone-value rule), and credential-based proof. Its voice is creator-economy hype and is not a model for anything written here.

Somewhere in your organization there is a renewal notice for an AI tool. Someone approved it, probably you. There was a demonstration that impressed the room, a pilot that was described as promising, and a vendor who said, kindly, that you were further along than most.

So when someone asks whether your business is ready for AI, the honest-sounding answer is yes. You have the licenses. You have the pilot. You have people who use it.

None of that answers the question. It answers a different one: whether you have bought AI. Readiness is something else, and the difference between the two is where most of the money spent on AI quietly disappears.

## Three leaders, three starting points

Picture three leaders who began the same year, with similar budgets and similar teams.

**The first one prepared.** Before buying anything, they wrote down the three problems in their work where AI would matter most, found out where the information behind those problems actually lived, and started small on the one with the highest return and the lowest risk. A year later, AI is part of how they work, and they can explain why in two sentences.

**The second one bought.** Tools arrived first, and the search for a problem came afterwards. The trials impressed everyone. A year later, nothing in this leader's own week works differently, and the licenses renew because cancelling them would feel like admitting something.

**The third one waited.** They agreed it mattered and handed it to someone more technical. A year later, things are being done with AI in their name, and they cannot judge whether any of it is good.

The second and third leaders are not careless. They are busy, and both of them made a choice that looked responsible at the time. That is what makes the pattern expensive: from the inside, buying and delegating both feel like progress.

## Why buying feels like readiness

Leaders hand their readiness to a purchase or to a person for understandable reasons. AI looks technical. It looks like a project. There is always someone who seems more at home with it.

Three things follow, and they follow almost every time.

**Tools arrive before a purpose.** The question becomes "what can we do with this?" instead of "what is this for?", and a tool without a purpose produces activity rather than results.

**Nobody knows the data.** Everyone assumes the information is in order until the first serious attempt to use it shows that it lives in chat threads, private spreadsheets and one long-serving person's memory. That is the problem behind [the advantage competitors cannot buy](/awakening/your-data-is-the-advantage-competitors-cannot-buy), and it is rarely visible until a tool runs into it.

**Experiments stay experiments.** A trial impresses, a report is written, and the daily work carries on exactly as before. It is the most common way [AI pilots go nowhere](/awakening/why-your-ai-pilot-went-nowhere), and it rarely looks like failure while it is happening.

> You are not ready for AI because you have bought AI. You are ready when you can say what it is for.

## Five things a ready leader can say

Readiness does not need a large budget, and it does not need you to become technical. It is a set of things you can say out loud, truthfully, and defend:

- I can name real problems in my work where AI would help.
- I know which of them to start with: high impact, low risk.
- The information those problems depend on is reliable, and I know where it lives.
- I have the skills, or the people, to take a good first result further than one trial.
- I decide which AI to use, and how, and I have thought about the consequences.

Notice that none of the five is about which tool you chose. Notice also that every one of them is about you, not about your IT department, your team or your vendor. That is deliberate. The organization's readiness follows the leader's. It rarely runs ahead of it.

If you are being asked by your board what your AI position is, these five are the material [the position your board expects you to have](/awakening/the-briefing-your-board-expects-you-to-have-had) is built from.

## Six questions worth asking yourself

Behind those five statements sit six questions. They are the six dimensions the AI Stakeholder Challenge uses to measure a leader's readiness, and each one asks something a purchase cannot answer for you.

| Dimension | The question, asked of yourself |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** | Do I have a plan for AI that is tied to where I am going, or is it still a topic? |
| **Data Governance** | Do I know what information my work runs on, where it is kept, and who answers for it? |
| **Talent** | Do I understand what AI can do for my work, and do I know what I do not yet know? |
| **Processes** | Is AI part of how my work actually gets done, or is it an experiment on the side? |
| **Impact Evaluation** | Do I decide in advance how I will know AI has helped, and what it might cost? |
| **Collaboration** | Do I work on this with people who see what I cannot, or alone? |

Two of these set the direction (Strategy and Impact Evaluation). Two are the foundation (Data Governance and Talent). Two decide whether the work moves (Processes and Collaboration). A leader can be strong in one group and weak in another, and that is exactly where the useful information is.

## Read the shape, not the score

Most readiness assessments you will find were built to measure an organization's technology. They ask dozens of technical questions a non-technical leader cannot honestly answer, add everything up into a single number, and often present that number as a verdict in red, with a product recommended as the cure.

A single number hides the thing you need to see. Two leaders can arrive at the same total from completely different places, and they need completely different first steps. What matters is the shape: which scores are high, which are low, and how far apart they sit.

A few shapes appear again and again.

**A strong plan resting on weak data.** You know where you want to go and have nothing to travel on. More strategy will not help. The first move is finding out what your information looks like.

**Good information with no purpose.** Your records are in order, and nobody has said what AI is for. The first move is the plan.

**Plenty of knowledge, worked alone.** You understand a great deal, and none of it reaches beyond you. The first move is the people you bring beside you.

**Busy, but unable to say whether it works.** AI is in the daily work, and nobody decided what a good result would look like. The first move is deciding how you will measure it.

<Callout label="A low score is a starting point">
A readiness profile is a picture of one moment, not a judgment of you as a leader. Its most useful output is not a grade. It is a precise answer to the question most leaders cannot currently answer: where do I start?
</Callout>

That is also why the measure has to ask what is true today rather than what you intend. A plan you are forming is not a plan. A trial you have promised is not a process. A tool open on your screen is not a strategy. Honest readiness is measured on what exists and what you have actually done, and then measured again a few months later, so the two pictures can sit side by side.

## The move you can make this week

You do not need an assessment to start. Take the last AI tool your organization paid for and write three sentences, in your own words, without asking anyone:

1. The problem it was bought to solve.
2. Where the information it depends on lives, and who answers for it being right.
3. How you would know, six months from now, that it had helped.

If all three come easily, you are closer to ready than the license count suggests. If the first one is hard, your gap is Strategy. If the second is hard, it is Data Governance. If the third is hard, it is Impact Evaluation, and [deciding how you will measure AI before you start](/awakening/decide-how-you-will-measure-ai-before-you-start) is where to begin. Each of those is a better use of your next month than another tool.

## STOP · 17 words

At your next board meeting somebody is going to ask about AI. Not the technology. The exposure.

## BELIEF · 57 words

And in your board packet AI still sits where it has always sat. Under innovation. A forward-looking slide, safely down the agenda, owned by whoever runs technology. Nobody has complained. It gets a nod, somebody asks a curious question, and the meeting moves on. That has been the pattern for two years and it has worked fine.

## TURN · 116 words

Except AI has crossed from the innovation agenda to the fiduciary one, and nobody sent a notice.

Not because a regulator said so on a particular Tuesday. Because of what fiduciary duty has always meant. Directors owe care and diligence on matters material to the organization. And AI now touches revenue, cost structure, workforce, customer trust and risk exposure at the same time, in most organizations, whether or not anyone at that table chose it. Three things made that true, and none of them needed a new law.

Which means the thing people usually get wrong. Materiality is not a decision the board gets to make. It is a condition the board is expected to notice.

## COST · 88 words

And one of those three should worry you more than the others.

Boards have now watched a generation of technology-driven governance failures. Data breaches. Algorithmic missteps. Model-driven decisions that harmed customers and reputations. Which means nobody sitting at a board table this year can credibly say AI risk was unforeseeable.

And foreseeability is what turns a surprise into a finding. The gap between those two words is the whole difference between bad luck and a failure of oversight, and it gets decided afterwards, by people reading minutes.

## MOVE · 70 words

So before the next agenda comes around, three things on one page. Where AI touches us today, including informally. Who is accountable for AI-assisted outcomes, by name. What we have decided AI must never be used for here.

If you sit on the management side, bring that yourself. Directors will get their AI clarity from someone. A consultant's deck, a peer's anecdote, or you. The briefing is in my bio.

## CLOSE · 34 words

A board does not need to understand AI the way an engineer does. It needs to understand it the way it understands debt. Well enough to govern how much, where, and on what terms.

## STOP · 15 words

Every function has sent you an AI line item this year. None called it capital.

## BELIEF · 60 words

And they all arrive the same way. A platform, a license, a pilot, a transformation program. Someone brings a deck with a capability on it and a number at the end. You evaluate it the way you evaluate any technology request. Is it plausible, is there budget, is the sponsor credible. That process has worked for twenty years of software.

## TURN · 118 words

But this is not a software cycle. Strip the vocabulary away and AI is a repricing event. The cost of certain categories of work, analysis, drafting, processing, reporting, monitoring, is falling steeply and unevenly across your industry.

Repricing events are not technology stories. They are capital allocation stories. And capital allocation is the question you already own.

Which changes what you are being asked. Not is this capability impressive. What recurring cost does this remove, and who is bleeding from it every week.

Fund the requests that name a specific friction with an owner attached. Decline the ones that name a technology and a vision, because those die on schedule while the subscription renews quietly on your budget.

## COST · 98 words

And the bigger number is not on any of those decks.

Somewhere in your industry a competitor is rebuilding a cost structure on the assumption that analysis and processing are cheap. When they get it right they will not announce it. It will show up as pricing you cannot match and margins you cannot explain.

So the strategic version of your question is this. Which of our costs does the industry still assume are fixed, and what happens to our position if somebody un-fixes them first? That belongs in this planning cycle, not the one after the evidence arrives.

## MOVE · 67 words

So, one thing this quarter. Find out what your organization actually spends on AI. Not the approved platforms. The seats, the add-ons, the personal-card subscriptions scattered across departmental budgets.

Most organizations spend more than any single person knows. Consolidating that number is unglamorous and it is the natural first move for the finance seat claiming this ground. The rest is in the briefing, linked in my bio.

## CLOSE · 36 words

Every organization is about to need an owner for the economics of AI. Not the technology. The economics. That job is unclaimed in most organizations, and it belongs to the seat that already owns capital discipline.

## Production notes

- **The STOP line is the on-screen caption.** Four-word sting, and it is the whole reframe compressed: "None called it capital." Beat before it.
- **STOP form is the count**, last used at row 2, nine rows back.
- **This is the first script written to a functional seat rather than a general leader.** The vocabulary is deliberately finance-native: repricing event, cost structure, planning cycle, capital discipline. That is not jargon here, it is the audience's own language, and using it correctly is the credibility test. Do not translate it down.
- **The re-hook is the first line of COST.**
- **The link is named once, in MOVE.** The close lands with no sign-off.
- **Bio link** carries `utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=briefing-the-cfos-ai-question-is-a-capital-question&utm_content=video`, set on `michaelsteve.com/links` for the run of this post.
- **Loop check:** the close lands on "already owns capital discipline", which runs into "Every function has sent you an AI line item" on a rewatch. Leave a beat of silence.
- The word "territory" is deliberately absent, replaced by "that job". It is our term, not theirs, and to a CFO it sounds like consulting language.
- COST is the only beat aimed outside the building. Everything else is about their desk; this one is about a competitor they cannot see yet. Slow down on "they will not announce it".

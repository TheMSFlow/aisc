## STOP · 15 words

The tool worked. The timing was fine. The team was willing. The pilot died anyway.

## BELIEF · 54 words

And the post-mortem said what post-mortems always say. Wrong tool. Wrong time. Team was not ready. Maybe nobody even ran one, the thing just went quiet, the meetings got shorter and then optional, and the subscription renewed unused for a year. Either way the conclusion was the same. Try again when the technology matures.

## TURN · 121 words

But look closely and the tool mostly worked, the timing was fine, and the team was willing. What was missing was never technical. Three things, and all three were visible on day one.

Nobody owned it. Somebody was assigned, which is not the same. Ownership means a person whose own standing rises or falls with the outcome. Assignment means an addition to a real job, and the moment the real job pressed, and it always presses, the pilot became the thing that could slip.

It solved nothing anyone was bleeding from. It started from the tool. Here is an impressive capability, where could we use it. That question selects for what demos well, not what hurts.

And there was no standard.

## COST · 94 words

And the third one is the quiet killer.

For AI output to enter real work, people need to know what good looks like, what gets checked, and who answers if it is wrong. Most pilots launch with none of that.

So half the team trusts it too much, and the first visible error poisons the well. The other half never trusts it at all, does every task twice, and correctly concludes the pilot made their week longer.

Adoption is not an enthusiasm problem. It is a trust problem, and trust is built from standards.

## MOVE · 68 words

So before the next one launches, three sentences on one page. Who owns this, whose standing moves with it. What friction it removes, and who bleeds from that every week. What good output looks like, who checks it, who answers if it is wrong.

If you cannot write all three, do not launch. The reason they are hard to write is in the briefing, linked in my bio.

## CLOSE · 31 words

Your next pilot does not need a better vendor. It needs those three filled in before launch, and the hardest one to fill is the one sitting in the sponsor's chair.

## Production notes

- **The STOP line is the on-screen caption.** Three short affirmations then the reversal. Beat before "The pilot died anyway", and do not let the voice go sad on it. Flat is funnier and lands harder.
- **STOP form is the flat contradiction**, last used at row 4, far enough back.
- **TURN deliberately withholds the third absence.** "And there was no standard" ends the beat with no explanation, and COST picks it up. That is the only structural tease in any script in the set, and it is legitimate because the payoff arrives eight seconds later inside the same video, not behind a link.
- **The re-hook is the first line of COST.**
- **The link is named once, in MOVE.** The close lands with no sign-off.
- **Bio link** carries `utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=briefing-why-your-ai-pilot-went-nowhere&utm_content=video`, set on `michaelsteve.com/links` for the run of this post.
- **Loop check:** the close lands on "the one sitting in the sponsor's chair", which runs into "The tool worked" on a rewatch. Leave a beat of silence.
- The close is the only place the sponsor gets implicated, and it is deliberately last. Naming it earlier turns the whole script into an accusation and the viewer stops hearing the diagnosis.
